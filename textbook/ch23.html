Understanding diseases of the breast requires knowledge of normal a breast anatomy and the dynamic structural changes that occur within the breast parenchyma throughout life. The cellular constituents of the breast include two major structures (ducts and lobules), two types of epithelial cells (luminal and myoepithelial), and two types of stromal cells (interlobular and intralobular). 01Disorders of Development

Milk Line Remnants

Supernumerary nipples or breasts result from the persistence of epidermal thickenings along the milk line, which extends from the axilla to the perineum. Breast disorders only very rarely arise in these heterotopic foci, which most commonly come to attention due to painful swelling prior to menstruation.

Accessory Axillary Breast Tissue

In some females the normal ductal system extends into the subcutaneous tissue of the chest wall or the axillary fossa (the axillary tail of Spence). Because this tissue may not be removed by prophylactic mastectomies, such procedures markedly reduce, but do not eliminate, the risk of breast cancer.

Congenital Nipple Inversion

The failure of the nipple to evert during development is common and may be unilateral. Congenitally inverted nipples are usually of little significance since they correct spontaneously during pregnancy or may sometimes be everted by simple traction. Acquired nipple retraction is of more concern since it may indicate the presence of an invasive cancer or an inflammatory nipple disease.

02Clinical Presentations of Breast Disease

The most common breast signs and symptoms reported by females are “lumpiness,” pain, palpable mass, nipple discharge, and inflammatory changes (Fig. 23.2A). Symptoms are usually not severe enough to require treatment and the primary reason for investigating their cause is to evaluate the possibility of malignancy. Greater than 90% of symptomatic breast lesions are benign. Of females with cancer, about 45% have symptomatic disease, while the remainder come to attention through mammographic screening (Fig. 23.2B). The principal mammographic signs of breast carcinoma are densities and calcifications. Among the most common or significant signs and symptoms that bring breast lesions to clinical attention are:

  • Lumpiness, or diffuse nodularity of the breast, is usually a manifestation of normal glandular tissue or fibrocystic changes. When pronounced, imaging studies may be needed to exclude the presence of a discrete mass.
  • Pain (mastalgia or mastodynia) is a common symptom that may be cyclic with menses or noncyclic. Diffuse cyclic pain may be due to premenstrual edema. Noncyclic pain is usually localized to one area of the breast and may be caused by a ruptured cyst, physical injury, or infection, but often no specific lesion is identified.

Although most painful masses are benign, in about 5% of cases the underlying cause is breast cancer.

  • Palpable masses may arise from proliferations of stromal cells or epithelial cells and are generally detected when 2 to 3 cm in size.

Most (,95%) are benign; these tend to be discrete masses that are round or oval, rubbery, and mobile. The most common benign masses are fibroadenomas and cysts. In contrast, because malignant tumors invade across tissue planes they tend to be irregular, hard (scirrhous), and nonmobile. The likelihood that a mass is malignant increases with age, rising from 10% for females under age 40 to 60% for females over age 50. Approximately 50% of carcinomas occur in the upper outer quadrant, 10% in each of the remaining quadrants, and about 20% in the central or subareolar region. Although about one-third of cancers are first detected as a palpable mass, screening by palpation has little effect on breast cancer mortality, as most cancers with the capacity to metastasize do so before they reach a palpable size.

  • Nipple discharge may be normal when small in quantity and bilateral. Milky discharge (galactorrhea) is associated with elevated prolactin levels (e.g., due to pituitary adenoma), hypothyroidism, and endocrine anovulatory syndromes, and may also occur in patients taking oral contraceptives, tricyclic antidepressants, methyldopa, or phenothiazines. Repeated nipple stimulation may also induce lactation. Galactorrhea is not a feature of malignancy. Bloody or serous discharges are most commonly due to large duct papillomas and cysts. During pregnancy, rapid growth and remodeling of the breast may produce a bloody discharge. Nipple discharge in older females that is spontaneous, unilateral, and bloody is likely to be due to malignancy Discharge associated with malignancy is most commonly due to ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and increases in prevalence with age; it is caused by an underlying carcinoma in 7% of females under age 60 years and in 30% of females 60 years of age or older.
  • Inflammation causes erythema and edema involving all or part of a breast. This is most often caused by infections, which only occur with any frequency during lactation and breastfeeding.

03Inflammatory Disorders

Inflammatory diseases of the breast are rare (accounting for less than 1% of breast symptoms) and are caused by infections, autoimmune disease, or foreign body–type reactions to extravasated keratin or secretions. Inflammatory breast cancer mimics inflammation clinically and should always be considered in a female with an erythematous swollen breast.

Acute Mastitis

Acute bacterial mastitis typically occurs during the first month of breastfeeding (lactational mastitis) and is caused by a local infection when the breast is most vulnerable due to cracks and fissures in the nipples. From this portal of entry, Staphylococcus aureus or less commonly streptococci invade the breast tissue. The breast is erythematous and painful, and fever is often present. At the outset only one duct system or sector of the breast is involved. If not treated, the infection may spread to the entire breast. Staphylococcal infection often leads to single or multiple abscesses, whereas streptococci cause spreading infection in the form of cellulitis. Most cases of lactational mastitis are effectively treated with appropriate antibiotics and continued expression of milk from the breast. Only rarely is surgical drainage required.

Squamous Metaplasia of Lactiferous Ducts

Squamous metaplasia of lactiferous ducts is known by a variety of names, including recurrent subareolar abscess, periductal mastitis, and Zuska disease. Females (and sometimes males) present with a painful erythematous subareolar mass that mimics a bacterial abscess. In recurrent cases, a fistula tract often develops under the smooth muscle of the nipple and opens onto the skin at the edge of the areola. In many affected females the nipple inverts due to traction produced by inflammation and scarring.

More than 90% of afflicted individuals are smokers. It has been suggested that a relative deficiency of vitamin A associated with toxic substances in tobacco smoke alters the differentiation of the ductal epithelium.

Morphology

The key feature is keratinizing squamous metaplasia, which extends into the nipple duct well past the usual point of transition from squamous to glandular epithelium (Fig. 23.3). Keratin shed from these cells is trapped and plugs affected ducts, causing ductal dilation and eventual rupture. An intense chronic granulomatous inflammatory response develops once keratin spills into the surrounding periductal tissue. With recurrences, secondary anaerobic bacterial infections may supervene and cause acute inflammation.

Simple incision drains the abscess cavity, but the offending keratinizing epithelium remains and recurrences are common. In most cases, en bloc surgical removal of the involved duct and contiguous fistula tract is curative. If secondary bacterial infection is present, antibiotics also have a therapeutic role.

Duct Ectasia

Duct ectasia presents as a palpable periareolar mass that is often associated with thick, white nipple secretions and occasionally with skin retraction. Pain and erythema are uncommon. This disorder tends to occur in the fifth or sixth decade of life in multiparous females. Unlike squamous metaplasia of lactiferous ducts, it is not associated with cigarette smoking.

Morphology

Ectatic dilated ducts are filled with inspissated secretions and numerous lipid-laden macrophages. Duct rupture stimulates a periductal and interstitial chronic inflammatory reaction consisting of lymphocytes, macrophages, and variable numbers of plasma cells (Fig. 23.4). Granulomas may form around cholesterol deposits and secretions. Subsequent fibrosis produces an irregular mass with skin and nipple retraction. The principal significance of duct ectasia is that it may be difficult to distinguish on clinical and radiologic grounds from invasive carcinoma.

Fat Necrosis

The presentations of fat necrosis may closely mimic cancer. It may come to attention as a painless palpable mass, skin thickening or retraction, or mammographic densities or calcifications. About half of affected females have a history of breast trauma or surgery.

Morphology

Acute lesions may be hemorrhagic and contain central areas of liquefactive fat necrosis with neutrophils and macrophages. Over subsequent days, proliferating fibroblasts and chronic inflammatory cells surround the injured area; giant cells, calcifications, and hemosiderin then appear, and eventually the lesion is replaced by scar tissue or is encircled and walled off by fibrous tissue. Ill-defined, firm, gray-white nodules containing small chalky-white foci are seen grossly.

Lymphocytic Mastopathy (Sclerosing Lymphocytic Lobulitis)

This condition presents with single or multiple hard palpable masses or mammographic densities. The masses are associated with areas of densely collagenized stroma, a feature that may make it difficult to obtain lesional tissue by needle biopsy. Within the stroma are atrophic ducts and lobules with thickened basement membranes surrounded by a lymphocytic infiltrate. This condition is most common in females with type 1 diabetes (diabetic mastopathy) or Hashimoto thyroiditis and is hypothesized to have an autoimmune basis. Its only clinical significance is that it must be distinguished from breast cancer.

Granulomatous Mastitis

Granulomatous inflammation of the breast may be a manifestation of systemic granulomatous diseases (e.g., granulomatosis with polyangiitis, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis) or infectious disorders localized to the breast. Granulomatous lobular mastitis is an uncommon disease that occurs only in parous females. The granulomas are closely associated with lobules and may contain lipid vacuoles surrounded by neutrophils.

A similar histologic pattern is seen in cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis, which is often caused by lipophilic Corynebacteria. These histologic patterns may be manifestations of the same disease. Treatment includes antibiotics and sometimes steroids. Localized infections by mycobacteria or fungi are rare and are most common in immunocompromised patients or adjacent to foreign objects such as breast prostheses or nipple piercings.

Key concepts
  • Inflammatory diseases of the breast are rare outside of the lactational period.
  • The specific cause must be determined, as appropriate treatment may be antibiotics, steroids, or surgery.
  • The possibility of inflammatory carcinoma mimicking a nonneoplastic inflammatory disorder should always be considered.
  • Majority of cases of periductal mastitis are associated with smoking, which is thought to damage the ducts.
  • Granulomatous lobular mastitis is often idiopathic.

04Benign Epithelial Proliferations and Precursor Lesions

Most benign epithelial lesions arise in the terminal duct lobular unit and can be classified into three groups, based on the magnitude of risk for subsequent development of breast cancer: (1) nonproliferative breast changes, (2) proliferative breast disease without atypia, and (3) proliferative breast disease with atypia (atypical hyperplasia). Most come to clinical attention when detected by mammography or as incidental findings in surgical specimens.

Nonproliferative Breast Changes (Fibrocystic Changes)

This group includes common morphologic alterations that are often grouped under the term fibrocystic changes. These changes occur most often between 30 and 50 years of age, possibly caused by hormonal changes during menstruation. Historically, the term “nonproliferative” denotes breast changes not associated with an increased risk of breast cancer; however, this name is somewhat misleading, as some of these changes involve increased proliferation and may even be associated with clonal genetic aberrations.

Morphology

Three principal morphologic features fall under fibrocystic changes:

(1) cystic change, often with apocrine metaplasia; (2) fibrosis; and (3) adenosis.

  • Fibrosis. Cysts frequently rupture, releasing secretory material into the adjacent stroma. The resulting chronic inflammation and fibrosis produce palpable nodularity of the breast.
  • Adenosis. Adenosis is defined as an increase in the number of acini per lobule. The acini are lined by columnar epithelial cells, and calcifications are occasionally present within the lumens.

Another “nonproliferative” morphological alteration is lactational adenoma, which presents as a palpable mass in pregnant or lactating females and regresses after cessation of breastfeeding. Lactational adenomas consist of normal-appearing breast tissue with lactational changes and may be an exaggerated local response to gestational hormones rather than true neoplasms.

Proliferative Breast Disease Without Atypia

Lesions characterized by proliferation of epithelial cells, without atypia, are associated with a small increase in the risk of subsequent carcinoma in both breasts. They are commonly detected as mammographic densities, calcifications, or incidental findings in biopsies performed for other reasons. These lesions are considered predictors of risk rather than direct precursors of carcinoma.

Morphology

Several morphologically distinct patterns of proliferative breast disease without atypia are recognized:

  • Usual ductal hyperplasia. Compared to normal terminal duct lobular units (Fig. 23.6A and C), there is an increased number of both luminal and myoepithelial cells filling and distending ducts and lobules (Fig. 23.6B and D). Irregular lumens can often be seen at the periphery of the cellular masses. Usual ductal hyperplasia is typically an incidental finding.
  • Sclerosing adenosis. This is a glandular proliferation characterized by an increased number of acini that are compressed and distorted by stromal fibrosis. On occasion, stromal fibrosis completely compresses the lumens to create the appearance of solid cords or double strands of cells lying within dense stroma, a pattern that superficially resembles invasive carcinoma (Fig. 23.7). Sclerosing adenosis may come to attention as a palpable mass, a radiologic density, or calcifications.
  • Radial scar (radial sclerosing lesion). This small stellate lesion closely mimics invasive carcinoma mammographically, grossly, and histologically (Fig. 23.8). The term radial scar is a misnomer as it is not associated with prior trauma or surgery. These lesions have a central nidus of entrapped glands in a hyalinized elastotic stroma surrounded by long radiating projections of glandular elements that may have components of sclerosing adenosis, cysts, papilloma, and usual ductal hyperplasia. Complex sclerosing lesion is defined as a radial scar larger than 1 cm in size.
  • Papilloma. Papillomas grow within a dilated duct and are composed of multiple branching fibrovascular cores (Fig. 23.9). Usual ductal hyperplasia and apocrine metaplasia are frequently present within the lesion. Large duct papillomas occur centrally in the lactiferous sinuses of the nipple and are usually solitary. Small duct papillomas are commonly multiple and located within the peripheral ductal system. More than 80% of large duct papillomas produce nipple discharge, which may be bloody due to torsion of the stalk, leading to infarction. Serous discharge results from intermittent blockage and release of normal breast secretions or irritation of the duct by the papilloma. Most small duct papillomas are clinically occult but they may come to attention as small palpable masses or mammographic densities or calcifications.

Gynecomastia

Gynecomastia (enlargement of the male breast) is the only benign lesion seen with any frequency in the male breast. It presents as a button-like subareolar enlargement and may be unilateral or bilateral. Microscopically, there is an increase in dense collagenous connective tissue associated with epithelial hyperplasia of the duct lining (Fig. 23.10). Lobule formation is almost never observed.

Gynecomastia occurs due to an imbalance between estrogens, which stimulate breast tissue, and androgens, which counteract these effects. It may appear during puberty, in the very aged, or at any time during adult life when there is cause for hyperestrinism. The most important of these is cirrhosis of the liver, the organ responsible for metabolizing estrogen. In older males, gynecomastia may stem from a relative increase in estrogens as testicular androgen production falls.

Drugs such as alcohol, marijuana, heroin, antiretroviral therapy, and anabolic steroids have been associated with gynecomastia. Rarely, gynecomastia occurs as part of Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY karyotype) or in association with functioning testicular neoplasms, such as Leydig cell or Sertoli cell tumors.

Breast cancer precursor lesions are noninvasive neoplastic epithelial proliferations with the biologic potential to progress to malignancy.

They typically demonstrate the same morphologic and molecular features as subsequent invasive carcinoma.

Columnar cell lesions and flat epithelial atypia are clonal proliferations that are thought to be the earliest morphologically recognizable precursors within the low-grade breast neoplasia pathway (discussed later). They typically present as calcifications on mammographic screening or may be an incidental finding in surgical specimens.

Atypical hyperplasia is a clonal proliferation having some, but not all, of the histologic features of low-grade carcinoma in situ. It is divided into two forms, atypical ductal hyperplasia and atypical lobular hyperplasia. Atypical ductal hyperplasia is present in approximately 10% of biopsies performed to evaluate calcifications. Atypical lobular hyperplasia is an incidental finding and is found in fewer than 5% of biopsies.

Morphology
  • Columnar cell lesions are characterized by variably dilated acini lined by columnar epithelial cells, frequently associated with microcalcifications.
  • Flat epithelial atypia is a columnar cell lesion with cytologic atypia that is distinguished from atypical ductal hyperplasia by the absence of architectural complexity.
  • Atypical ductal hyperplasia is a monomorphic proliferation of regularly spaced epithelial cells that may be arranged in cribriform or micropapillary patterns, resembling low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS). It is distinguished from DCIS by its limited extent and small size (Fig. 23.11A).
  • Atypical lobular hyperplasia is a neoplastic proliferation of dyscohesive epithelial cells confined to the ductal-lobular system that is histologically identical to classic lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) but more limited in extent (Fig. 23.11B). Columnar cell lesions, flat epithelial atypia, and atypical hyperplasia express high levels of estrogen receptor (ER), have low rates of proliferation, and often have the same acquired chromosomal aberrations that are found in low-grade carcinoma in situ and well differentiated ER-positive invasive breast cancer. Atypical lobular hyperplasia also shows loss of E-cadherin expression, a feature shared with LCIS and invasive lobular carcinoma (discussed later).

The major precursor lesion of high-grade invasive carcinoma is high-grade DCIS (discussed later). Early histologic precursors of high-grade DCIS have yet to be defined. Clinical Significance of Benign Epithelial Changes

Epidemiologic studies have established associations between benign histologic changes and the later development of invasive cancer (Table 23.1). Both breasts are at increased risk. Most patients diagnosed with atypical ductal hyperplasia on core needle biopsy are treated with surgical excision, which reveals DCIS or invasive carcinoma in approximately 10% to 20% of cases. Management of other precursor lesions, including flat epithelial atypia and atypical lobular hyperplasia, usually entails close clinical and radiological surveillance. Risk reduction can also be achieved with estrogen antagonists, such as tamoxifen.

In contrast, high-grade DCIS may be ER-negative and/or HER2-positive and shows molecular alterations (genomic instability, chromosomal changes) seen in high-grade invasive carcinomas.

Clinical Features

The natural history of DCIS has been difficult to determine because once detected it is typically treated, but the available data suggest that Scale crust on females with small, low-grade DCIS develop invasive cancer at a rate of about 1% per year. When invasive cancer develops in the same breast quadrant, its grade and pattern of ER and HER2 expression usually resembles the associated DCIS. Patients with high-grade or extensive DCIS are believed to be at higher risk of invasive carcinoma and more rapid progression.

Most females with Paget disease without an underlying palpable mass have DCIS and are staged as such. However, around one-half of females with Paget disease have a palpable breast mass, which usually proves to be invasive carcinoma. Prognosis depends on the features of the invasive carcinoma (if present) and is not affected by the presence or absence of Paget disease.

Remarkably, in the United States the overall death rate for females with DCIS is lower than the population as a whole, possibly because mammographic screening is a “marker” for better access to medical care or other socioeconomic factors that are associated with longevity. Death from metastatic breast cancer after a diagnosis of DCIS occurs in 1% to 3% of females. The origin of metastatic disease may be from a subsequent invasive carcinoma in the ipsilateral or contralateral breast or occult foci of invasion that were not detected at the time of DCIS diagnosis.

Mastectomy is curative in greater than 95% of females with DCIS. The major risk factors for recurrence are (1) high nuclear grade and necrosis, (2) extensive disease, and (3) positive surgical margins. Ensuring complete excision of DCIS is not straightforward since its distribution in the breast is not reliably predicted by imaging and it is usually not grossly evident at surgery. Postoperative radiation therapy and tamoxifen also reduce the risk of recurrence.

Trials to identify patients who can safely undergo observation rather than treatment are ongoing.

Lobular Carcinoma In Situ

LCIS is a neoplastic proliferation of epithelial cells within ducts and lobules that grow in a dyscohesive fashion. It is almost always an incidental biopsy finding since it is rarely associated with calcifications or stromal reactions that produce mammographic densities. As a result, the incidence of LCIS (1% to 6% of all carcinomas) has been unchanged by mammographic screening. When both breasts are biopsied, LCIS is bilateral in 20% to 40% of cases, as compared with 10% to 20% of cases of DCIS.

The cells of atypical lobular hyperplasia, LCIS, and invasive lobular carcinoma are morphologically identical. The dyscohesion of the cells is usually due to loss-of-function of E-cadherin, a transmembrane cell adhesion protein expressed by epithelial cells in the breast and other glandular tissues. Rarely, other proteins that are needed for E-cadherin–mediated cellular adhesion, such as -catenin, are dysfunctional instead of E-cadherin. E-cadherin and associated proteins act as tumor suppressors in glandular tissues and may be lost in neoplastic proliferations through a variety of mechanisms, most commonly mutation or deletion of the E-cadherin gene CDH1 on chromosome 16q22.1. LCIS shares common genomic alterations (e.g., 16q deletion) with other lesions in the low-grade breast neoplasia pathway (discussed later) and demonstrates the same spectrum of mutations as synchronous invasive carcinoma.

Classically, LCIS consists of a uniform population of epithelial cells with oval or round nuclei and small nucleoli that fill and expand more than half of the acini in a terminal duct lobular unit (Fig. 23.14A). Mucin-positive signet ring cells are commonly present. Loss of E-cadherin expression (Fig. 23.14B) produces rounded cells that lack attachments to adjacent cells. Pagetoid spread, defined by the presence of neoplastic cells between the basement membrane and overlying luminal cells, is commonly seen in breast ducts, but Paget disease (involvement of nipple skin) is rare. Necrosis and secretory activity are generally not seen in classic LCIS and thus calcifications are typically absent. However, aggressive variants of LCIS (e.g., pleomorphic type) may be associated with calcifications and necrosis.

LCIS almost always expresses ER and progesterone receptor (PR) and is usually HER2-negative.

Clinical Features

LCIS is a risk factor for developing invasive carcinoma in either breast, with a slightly higher risk to the ipsilateral breast. Compared to the general population, patients with classic LCIS have an 8- to 10-fold increased risk of invasive carcinoma, which develops at a rate of about 1% per year, a rate similar to that of untreated low-grade DCIS. Unlike DCIS, it is unclear whether surgical removal of LCIS lowers risk. Classic LCIS identified on core biopsy may be managed with active surveillance, given the very low probability of identifying an associated invasive carcinoma in cases with concordant imaging findings. More aggressive variants of LCIS (e.g., pleomorphic) are managed with surgical excision. Other treatment strategies include mastectomy or endocrine therapy in select cases.

Key concepts
  • There are two types of carcinoma in situ, ductal and lobular, that are distinguished on the basis of histologic features and E-cadherin protein expression.
  • Both DCIS and LCIS arise within the terminal duct lobular unit.
  • DCIS is graded according to nuclear features rather than architectural pattern.
  • DCIS is treated locally, as subsequent invasive carcinomas usually occur at the same site, whereas LCIS confers bilateral risk.

05Invasive Carcinoma of the Breast

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy globally and the leading cause of cancer death in females worldwide. In 2020, breast cancer surpassed lung cancer as the most common cancer overall with an estimated 2.3 million new cases, accounting for almost 12% of new cancer diagnoses across the world. The continued rise in the worldwide incidence of breast cancer is mainly attributable to social and lifestyle factors; however, improvements in survival outcomes have been attained, mainly in higher resourced countries, through earlier diagnosis and the development of more effective adjuvant therapy.

Breast cancer is a heterogenous disease that encompasses numerous tumor subtypes defined by distinct clinical, morphological, and molecular phenotypes. Several approaches can be used to subclassify breast cancer into clinically meaningful subgroups (Fig. 23.15). At diagnosis, morphological assessment delineates in situ from invasive disease and informs histological tumor type, grade, and anatomic stage. The routine evaluation of three biomarkers—ER, PR, and HER2—separates tumors into three major clinical groups, ER-positive/HER2-negative (“luminal”), HER2-positive (“HER2”), and ER/PR/HER2-negative (triple negative breast cancer, “TNBC”), which differ with respect to tumor biology, natural history, and treatment response.

However, characterization of morphology and ER, PR, and HER2 expression do not come close to capturing the full molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer. A useful molecular classification system that better reflects the spectrum of breast cancer biology and behavior separates breast cancers into six major “intrinsic” subtypes based on gene expression profiling; luminal A, luminal B, HER2 enriched, basal-like, normal-like, and claudin-low. Because these molecular subtypes correlate reasonably well with ER and HER2 protein expression, which is easily assessed by standard clinical assays, molecular subtyping by expression profiling is not a routine part of breast cancer classification. However, gene expression signatures provide predictive and prognostic information and several assays based on gene expression have been developed that help guide treatment decisions in certain clinical contexts.

Incidence and Epidemiology

The lifetime risk of developing breast cancer is one in eight for females living to age 90 years in the United States. In 2023, it is estimated that 297,790 females in the United States will be diagnosed with invasive breast cancer, and more than 43,000 females will die of the disease—a toll among cancers second only to lung cancer. Incidence rates vary geographically, with the highest incidence reported in North America, Northern and Western Europe, and Australia/New Zealand. However, breast cancer incidence and mortality are increasing at an alarming rate in many low-resource countries. The factors underlying this trend are thought to be social changes that increase breast cancer risk—specifically, delayed childbearing, fewer pregnancies, and reduced breastfeeding—combined with late presentation and a lack of access to optimal health care.

Breast cancer is rare in females younger than age 25 years and increases in incidence rapidly after age 30 years (Fig. 23.16). While the incidence of TNBC and HER2 cancer plateaus in middle age (,40 years), the incidence of luminal cancer continues to rise and peaks later in life.

As a result, TNBCs and HER2 cancers account for almost half of breast cancers in young females and fewer than 20% of cancers in older females.

The 1980s saw a marked increase in the apparent incidence of breast cancer in older females due to two factors: (1) the introduction of mammographic screening, leading to increased cancer detection; and (2) the increased use of postmenopausal hormonal therapy. After 2000, the number of newly diagnosed breast cancers fell in older females, probably due to a plateauing of the rate of mammographic screening (approximately 65% to 75% of eligible females) and a decline in the use of postmenopausal hormone therapy. In contrast, over the same time the incidence of breast cancer remained relatively constant in younger females.

Mammography is used to detect nonpalpable asymptomatic breast carcinomas and is the major screening test for breast cancer. The principal mammographic signs (none of which are specific) of breast carcinoma are densities, architectural distortions, and calcifications. The average size of invasive carcinoma detected by mammography is about 1 cm (significantly smaller than carcinomas detected by palpation). The sensitivity and specificity of mammography increases with age. At age 40 years, the probability that a mammographic lesion is cancer is only 10% but this rises to greater than 25% in females older than 50 years. Approximately 10% of invasive carcinomas are not visible on standard, two-dimensional mammographic images. Other imaging modalities, including digital breast tomosynthesis, ultrasonography, and magnetic resonance imaging, can be useful adjuncts in such circumstances.

Breast cancer incidence and biology vary with ethnicity (Fig. 23.16). The incidence of breast cancer is highest in females of European descent; in this group, the average age at diagnosis is 63 years, and only 20% of cases are diagnosed before age 50 years. By contrast, the average age at diagnosis for females of African descent is 59 years, and 35% of cancers are diagnosed at ages below 50 years. For Hispanic females, the average age at diagnosis is 56, and 20% are diagnosed at ages below 50 years. The “excess” cancers in females of European descent are mostly of the luminal type. Thus, TNBCs and HER2 cancers make up a greater proportion of cancers occurring in other ethnic groups.

The risk of death in those who develop invasive breast cancer has gradually declined, most recently by 1% to 2% per year; the current overall risk of death is about 20%. However, the decline in the death rate has lagged in females of African descent, who have the highest mortality rate. Although this difference is explained partly by unequal access to care, breast cancers in females of African descent are also more likely to be biologically aggressive (e.g., TNBC) and to fall into molecular subtypes that are difficult to treat.

Pathogenesis of Breast Cancer

Like other cancers, breast cancers arise through several pathways that involve the stepwise acquisition of driver mutations in epithelial cells. The mutation that initiates the process, as demonstrated by inherited cancers (discussed later), appears to strongly influence the phenotype of the cancer that ultimately develops, as may the specific cell type in which the initiating event occurs. Low-grade and high-grade breast carcinomas differ in their molecular profiles and are thought to evolve along largely separate pathways from distinct precursor lesions (Fig. 23.17).

The low-grade breast neoplasia pathway proceeds through recognizable ER-positive precursor lesions, including columnar cell lesions, flat epithelial atypia (i.e., columnar cell lesions with atypia), atypical hyperplasia (ductal and lobular), low-grade DCIS, and classic LCIS, which share the potential to progress to low-grade ER-positive invasive carcinoma. At the genetic level, this pathway is defined by specific genomic alterations, such as loss of 16q and gain of 1q, and a predominantly “luminal” gene expression signature. The major precursor lesion of high-grade invasive carcinoma is high-grade DCIS. The lesions that constitute the earliest steps in the high-grade breast neoplasia pathway have yet to be identified.

Common alterations associated with the high-grade pathway include loss of 13q, gain of 11q13, amplification of 17q12, and a gene expression signature enriched for genes that are highly expressed in proliferating cells.

As with other carcinomas that begin as in situ lesions, it is not clear what drives the transition from in situ to invasive breast carcinoma.

Most genomic changes observed in invasive carcinoma are already present in noninvasive precursor lesions, including major driver mutations. Indeed, genomic changes specific to invasive carcinoma have not been identified, suggesting that invasion may depend on other factors, such as changes in the tumor microenvironment, rather than in the tumor cells.

There is an increasing awareness that the tumor microenvironment (stromal cells, immune cells, and extracellular matrix) plays an important role in breast cancer initiation, progression, and dissemination. Maintenance of the basement membrane depends on the interaction of luminal cells, myoepithelial cells, and stromal cells in the local microenvironment. In some in situ cancers, myoepithelial cells are reduced in number and the associated basement membrane is thin and has gaps, which may set the stage for stromal invasion by breast cancer cells.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts may contribute to tumor progression by facilitating cell adhesion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immunosuppression. Breast cancer cells can suppress the host immune response through several mechanisms, including the upregulation of certain cell surface ligands such as the immune checkpoint protein programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) (Chapter 7). Through these types of dynamic interactions, cancer cells can create a “tumor promoting” microenvironment that allows them to evade the host immune response. We now turn to a more in-depth discussion of the major molecular pathways involved in the pathogenesis of familial and sporadic breast cancer.

Pathogenesis of Familial Breast Cancer

It is believed that 25% to 35% of breast cancers occur due to the inheritance of a variant in a susceptibility gene or genes. Single gene mutations with moderate to high penetrance (“penetrance” refers to the risk of an individual with the mutated gene developing cancer) accounts for 8% to 17% of breast carcinoma (Table 23.3). Inheritance is also thought to play a role in an additional 15% to 20% of females based on a positive family history, defined as breast cancer in a first-degree relative (mother, sister, or daughter), in multiple relatives, or with onset at an early age. In these females, inheritance of a single susceptibility gene variant with low penetrance or combinations of polymorphic variants that interact is believed to explain increased risk. The most important high penetrance susceptibility genes for familial breast cancer are tumor suppressor genes that regulate genomic stability or cell growth (Table 23.3). Risk is inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion and stems from alleles with loss-of-function mutations.

As with other tumor suppressor genes, cancer development is associated with acquired loss-of-function of the remaining normal allele (e.g., by mutation, deletion, or methylation-dependent silencing), leading to biallelic inactivation of the susceptibility gene.

Mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 are responsible for 80% to 90% of single gene familial breast cancers and about 3% to 6% of all breast cancers. Penetrance, age of onset, and susceptibility to other types of cancers vary according to the specific BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutation that is inherited, but most female carriers develop breast cancer by the age of 70 years, and male BRCA2 carriers also have an increased risk of breast cancer. Mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 also increase the risk for other forms of epithelial cancer, particularly ovarian carcinoma (Chapter 22) and to a lesser degree prostatic and pancreatic carcinoma.

What is mysterious is why malfunction of these genes, particularly BRCA1 and BRCA2, is more highly associated with breast cancer than other cancers, as BRCA1 and BRCA2 are expressed ubiquitously throughout the body. It has also been suggested that estrogen may facilitate the survival of BRCA1-deficient cells. Finally, breast (and ovarian) epithelial cells may be particularly prone to sustaining the type of DNA damage that BRCA1 and BRCA2 are required to repair.

  • Estrogen regulates the expression of dozens of genes in breast epithelial cells that may directly contribute to tumor growth and development.
  • Estrogen stimulates the proliferation of breast epithelial cells during puberty, menstrual cycles, and pregnancy, thereby increasing the number of cells that are “at risk” for transformation. The DNA replication that attends cellular proliferation is conducive to the accumulation of mutations, and the lull in cell division that occurs during the latter part of the menstrual cycle may allow time for defective DNA repair to occur and for mutations to become “fixed” in the genome. Repeti-tion of this process during each cycle may underlie the association between the cumulative number of menstrual cycles a female experiences and her risk of developing breast cancer, as well as the strong association between luminal cancers and age (Fig. 23.16).

Clear evidence of the importance of estrogen is found in the therapeutic benefits of estrogen antagonists, which reduce the development of luminal cancers in females at high risk, and the increased incidence of luminal cancers in females treated with postmenopausal hormone therapy.

Types of Invasive Breast Carcinoma

Most breast malignancies are adenocarcinomas, which are subdivided into around 20 subtypes based on morphological appearances. The most common is invasive breast carcinoma of “no special type”, which accounts for approximately 75% of invasive breast carcinomas.

This heterogenous group of tumors is segregated from those belonging to “special” histologic types with distinct morphologic features, such as unique patterns of growth, tumor architecture, and cytomorphology.

We will first cover the morphologic features of invasive carcinomas of no special type and will then discuss several special subtypes of invasive carcinoma with informative pathogenic and clinical associations.

Invasive breast carcinoma of no special type. In the absence of mammographic screening, these carcinomas usually present as a mass of at least 2 to 3 cm in size. The mammographic and gross appearance varies widely depending on the stromal reaction to the tumor (Fig. 23.20). They most commonly present as a hard, irregular, radiodense mass (Fig. 23.20A and B) associated with a desmoplastic stromal reaction (Fig. 23.20C). When cut or scraped, such tumors typically produce a characteristic grating sound due to areas of chalky-white desmoplastic stroma and occasional foci of calcification. Less commonly, tumors present as a deceptively well-circumscribed mass composed of sheets of tumor cells with scant stromal reaction or alternatively may be almost imperceptible, being comprised of scattered neoplastic glands or single tumor cells infiltrating otherwise unremarkable fibrofatty tissue. Some invasive carcinomas of no special type are high-grade tumors with prominent populations of tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). These were previously considered a special subtype referred to as medullary carcinoma; however, this entity did not prove to be diagnostically reproducible, and such tumors are diagnosed in the current classification system as invasive carcinoma of no special type with “medullary pattern”.

Histologic grading. Grade refers to how closely the tumor resembles the normal structure of the breast (i.e., differentiation). All invasive carcinomas are graded using the Nottingham Histologic Score based on the degree of glandular differentiation (tubule formation), nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic rate. Grade 1 (well differentiated) carcinomas grow in a tubular or cribriform pattern and have small uniform nuclei and a low proliferative rate (Fig. 23.21A and D). Grade 2 (moderately differentiated) carcinomas have areas where cells grow as solid clusters or single infiltrating cells and show greater nuclear pleomorphism and higher numbers of mitotic figures (Fig. 23.21B and E). Grade 3 (poorly differentiated) carcinomas invade as ragged nests or solid sheets of cells and have enlarged irregular nuclei. A high proliferative rate and areas of tumor necrosis are common in high-grade tumors (Fig. 23.21C and F).

Special histologic Subtypes of breast cancer are morphologically distinct, often harbor unique genetic aberrations and gene signatures, and frequently show associations with clinical behavior and prognosis that differ from invasive carcinomas of no special type. These special tumors can be organized into molecular groups based on expression of ER, PR, and HER2, which carry their usual therapeutic implications. Although relatively uncommon, study of these tumors has provided important insights into breast cancer pathogenesis.

Invasive lobular carcinoma is a special subtype with clear genotype:phenotype associations. It is the second most common invasive breast cancer subtype, accounting for up to 15% of cases. In addition to its distinctive morphologic features (discussed later), lobular carcinomas have characteristic patterns of metastatic spread, often involving the peritoneum, retroperitoneum, leptomeninges (carcinomatous meningitis), gastrointestinal tract, ovaries, and uterus. Like LCIS, most cases show loss of expression of E-cadherin. Males and females with heterozygous germline mutations in CDH1 are at increased risk for developing lobular breast carcinoma as well as signet ring carcinoma of the stomach (Chapter 17).

Many other special histologic subtypes of breast cancer have been described and there is much that remains to be learned about their biology and pathogenesis. The morphologic features of some important special subtypes are discussed briefly.

Some special histologic subtypes of breast cancer almost always fall within the luminal (ER-positive/HER2-negative) group. These include invasive lobular carcinoma, mucinous carcinoma, tubular carcinoma, cribriform carcinoma, and papillary carcinoma. Invasive lobular carcinoma often infiltrates the breast insidiously while producing minimal desmoplasia, making it difficult to detect on imaging studies. The histologic hallmark is the presence of dyscohesive infiltrating tumor cells arranged in linear cords, often including signet ring cells containing intracytoplasmic mucin (Fig. 23.22A). Mucinous (colloid) carcinoma is soft or rubbery and gelatinous with pushing or circumscribed borders. The tumor cells are arranged in clusters of varying size within large lakes of mucin (Fig. 23.22B). Tubular carcinoma consists exclusively of well-formed tubules and is sometimes mistaken for a benign sclerosing lesion (Fig. 23.22C). Cribriform carcinoma consists of invasive nests with cribriform morphology. Papillary carcinoma produces true papillary fronds lined by tumor cells (Fig. 23.22D).

Inflammatory breast carcinoma is not a special histologic type but refers instead to a distinct clinical presentation caused by extensive plugging of the lymphovascular spaces of the dermis by carcinoma cells that leads to diffuse breast erythema, swelling, and skin thickening. The edematous skin is tethered to the breast by Cooper ligaments, producing skin dimpling that mimics the surface of an orange peel, an appearance referred to as peau d’orange. The presentation can be confused with infection, leading to delayed diagnosis; however, the name “inflammatory” is a misnomer, as typically no inflammation is present. The underlying carcinoma does not typically form a discrete palpable mass and is usually high-grade and diffusely infiltrative.

Clinical Presentation

Most invasive breast carcinomas present as a breast mass, detected either by palpation or mammographic screening. The mode of detection varies according to molecular subtype (Table 23.4). Cancers detected by mammographic screening are usually small luminal cancers limited to the breast, while HER2 positive cancers and TNBCs are aggressive and more likely to be detected as palpable masses. Larger carcinomas may invade the pectoralis muscle and become fixed to the chest wall or invade the dermis and cause retraction (dimpling) of the skin. When the tumor involves the central breast, retraction of the nipple may develop. Rarely, breast cancer presents as metastasis to an axillary lymph node or a distant site before cancer is detected in the breast. In such cases, the primary carcinoma may be small, obscured by dense breast tissue, or fail to produce a desmoplastic response. In most cases, these “occult” primary tumors (which are easily missed by palpation or mammography) are detected by imaging studies using ultrasound or MRI.

Prognostic and Predictive Factors

The outcome for females with breast cancer depends on the biologic features of the carcinoma (the molecular or histologic type) and the stage of disease at the time of diagnosis. Based on these factors, some females with breast cancer have a normal life expectancy, whereas others have only a 10% chance of being alive in 5 years. Patients who present at an advanced stage with distant metastasis (5% of patients) or with inflammatory carcinoma (1% to 5% of patients) have a particularly poor prognosis. For other cancers, prognosis is determined by pathologic evaluation of the primary tumor and the axillary lymph nodes, as well as the biomarker expression profile (Table 23.5).

Prognostic factors are important in counseling patients about the likely outcome of their disease, choosing the most appropriate treatment, and designing clinical trials. Predictive factors help to determine the likely response of a cancer to a specific type of treatment. Factors related to tumor biology are usually both prognostic and predictive (e.g., ER and HER2 expression, proliferation), whereas factors related to tumor extent (e.g., tumor size, lymph node metastases, distant metastases) are primarily prognostic. The major prognostic factors are (Table 23.5):

  • Distant metastases. Distant metastatic spread is the most important prognostic factor for invasive carcinoma. Once distant metastases are present, cure is unlikely, although long-term remissions and pallia-tion can be achieved, especially in females with ER-positive and HER2-positive tumors.
  • Lymph node metastases. Axillary lymph node status is the most important prognostic factor for invasive carcinoma in the absence of distant metastases. The clinical assessment of lymph node status is unreliable due to both false-positives (e.g., palpable reactive nodes) and false-negatives (e.g., lymph nodes with small metastatic deposits). Therefore, histological examination is necessary for accurate assessment. With no nodal involvement, the 10-year disease-free survival rate is 70% to 80%; the rate falls to 35% to 40% with one to three positive nodes and to 10% to 15% when more than 10 nodes are positive.

Lymphatic vessels in most breast carcinomas drain first to one or two sentinel nodes, which can be identified with radiotracer or colored dyes. If a biopsy of sentinel nodes is negative for metastasis, it is unlikely that other, more distant nodes will be involved; this approach is used to spare patients the morbidity of complete axillary dissection. Approximately 10% to 20% of females without axillary lymph node metastases experience recurrence with distant metastasis. In these patients, metastasis may occur via the internal mammary lymph nodes or hematogenously.

  • ER. ER is both a prognostic and predictive biomarker. ER-positive tumors (defined as $1% of tumor cells showing nuclear expression) respond to endocrine therapies, which reduce the rate of recurrence and improve survival outcomes. “Low positive” tumors (1% to 10% ER staining) appear to have a worse prognosis than tumors demonstrating high levels of ER expression.
  • PR. Breast cancers that are ER-positive are also typically PR-positive (defined as $1% of tumor cells showing nuclear expression). However, ER-positive tumors that show low levels of PR expression have worse clinical outcomes than tumors with high levels of both ER and PR.
  • HER2. HER2 is both a prognostic and predictive biomarker. HER2- positivity is an indicator of aggressive clinical behavior and response to HER2-targeting agents.
  • Gene expression profiling. Several proprietary assays that quantify mRNA levels in breast cancer cells have been developed. Most are heavily weighted toward inclusion of genes that are involved in proliferation. The greatest clinical value of these assays is to identify patients with slow-growing, ER-positive cancers who can be spared the toxicity of chemotherapy.
  • Response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Treating patients prior to surgery provides the opportunity to observe the tumor response to chemotherapy. One-third or more of TNBCs and HER2 cancers regress completely (termed “pathologic complete response”) following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Patients with responsive cancers belonging to these subtypes have a better prognosis than patients with nonresponsive cancers.
  • Tumor infiltrating lymphocytes. TILs are mononuclear immune cells (lymphocytes and plasma cells) infiltrating the tumor and its stroma. A prominent TIL component is associated with improved prognosis and better response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in HER2 and TNBC.
  • ESR1. Mutations in ESR1 (the gene that encodes ER) are major drivers of endocrine therapy resistance in ER-positive metastatic breast cancer, resulting in constitutive activation of ER and a worse prognosis. Testing for ESR1 mutations is recommended for ER-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer at the time of recurrence or progression on endocrine therapy to guide therapeutic decision making.
  • Predictive biomarkers for targeted therapies. Several biomarkers predict response to specific classes of drugs. Examples include the response to PARP inhibitors, which is predicted by the presence of germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations; the response of ER-positive/ HER2-negative breast cancer to PI3K inhibitors, which is predicted by the presence of PIK3CA mutations; and response of secretory breast carcinoma to tyrosine kinase inhibitors, which is predicted by the presence NTRK gene fusions. Similarly, response of breast carcinoma to specific immune checkpoint inhibitors is correlated with expression of PD-L1 in tumor and immune cells, tumor mutational burden (as assessed by NextGen sequencing), and the presence of mismatch repair deficiency (which is rare in breast cancer).
  • Circulating tumor DNA. Sequencing fragmented tumor-derived DNA shed into the blood by nonviable cancer cells can detect emerging actionable mutations for targeted therapy (e.g., PIK3CA mutations).

Breast Cancer Staging

The most important prognostic factors are included in the latest American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system, which integrates the anatomic stage and the molecular characteristics of individual breast cancers (Table 23.6). The anatomic stage (0 to IV) considers the primary tumor (T), involvement of regional lymph nodes (N), and the presence of distant metastases (M). The prognostic stage then takes into consideration histologic grade and biologic features (e.g., ER, PR, and HER2 status, as well as multigene panel results, if available) to ad-just the disease stage, with the aim of improving prognostic accuracy. For example, in some cases TNBC is “up-staged” in the new prognostic staging system to reflect its more aggressive behavior, while most luminal cancers are “down-staged.” The TNM staging system also considers clinical features that are associated with a worse prognosis, including inflammatory carcinoma, skin ulceration, and chest wall infiltration.

Key concepts
  • Breast cancer is the most common nonskin malignancy in females and the second most common cause of cancer deaths in the United States.
  • The most important risk factors for sporadic cancers in females are estrogenic stimulation and increasing age.
  • Approximately one-quarter to one-third of breast cancers are familial, being related to inheritance of genetic variants that increase breast cancer risk.
  • High-risk genes associated with familial breast cancer include several involved with DNA repair and genomic stability, most notably BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, and TP53.
  • The most common histologic subtype is invasive breast carcinoma of no special type, which accounts for around 75% of cases, while invasive lobular carcinoma is the most common special subtype.
  • Special histologic subtypes have prognostic importance and provide additional clues linking biologic changes to clinical behavior.
  • Breast cancers cluster into three major molecular groups, luminal (ER-positive), HER2, and TNBC, each with distinctive biologic and clinical features.
  • Luminal cancers are further divided into two groups, A and B, that differ mainly in terms of proliferation, which is low in group A and high in group B.
  • HER2 cancers are defined by overexpression of the HER2 receptor, usually due to HER2 gene amplification, and respond well to HER2 inhibitors.
  • TNBCs lack ER, PR, and HER2 expression, are often associated with defects in DNA repair and genomic stability (e.g., due to silencing of BRCA1 or TP53 mutation), and carry a relatively poor prognosis.
  • Prognosis is dependent on both biologic features and the extent of cancer at the time of diagnosis (anatomic stage).
  • In the AJCC eighth edition staging system, molecular group and anatomic stage are combined to create prognostic stage groups that provide better estimates of likely survival.
  • Effective treatment requires both local and systemic control of disease.
  • Improvements in treatment are being made as new targeted therapies are being developed and response to treatment is better understood.

06Stromal Tumors

The two types of stroma in the breast, intralobular and interlobular, give rise to distinct types of neoplasms. Two closely related breast-specific tumors, fibroadenoma and phyllodes tumor, arise from cells of the intralobular stroma. These tumors are termed “biphasic” because they also include a nonneoplastic epithelial component, the proliferation of which may be stimulated by growth factors elaborated by the stromal cells. By contrast, interlobular stroma is a source of the same types of mesenchymal tumors that occur in connective tissue elsewhere in the body (e.g., lipomas and angiosarcomas) as well as a few unusual stromal tumors that arise more commonly in the breast (e.g., myofibroblastoma).

Lesions of Intralobular Stroma

Fibroadenoma

Fibroadenoma is the most common benign stromal tumor of the female breast. Two-thirds of fibroadenomas harbor driver mutations in MED12, a gene that encodes a subunit of the mediator complex, which is involved in regulating the transcription of RNA polymerase II-dependent genes. The other tumor that is strongly associated with MED12 mutations, uterine leiomyoma, also arises from stromal cells within an organ that is responsive to female sex hormones. Clinical Features

Most fibroadenomas occur in females in their 20s and 30s. They are frequently multiple and bilateral. These tumors are hormonally responsive and may grow during pregnancy and regress after menopause. Rapid growth (sometimes leading to infarction) during pregnancy may raise a false suspicion of carcinoma. Curiously, almost half of females receiving cyclosporin A after renal transplantation develop multiple, bilateral fibroadenomas that regress after cessation of treatment.

Whether lesions associated with immunosuppression are true neoplasms or a form of reactive hyperplasia is unclear. Several subtypes of fibroadenomas are recognized, including myxoid and complex.

While most myxoid fibroadenomas are sporadic, a small proportion are associated with Carney complex, an inherited autosomal dominant condition usually caused by inactivating germline mutations in PRKAR1A. Complex fibroadenomas are defined by the presence of cysts larger than 0.3 cm, sclerosing adenosis, epithelial calcifications, or papillary apocrine change, and are associated with a slightly increased risk of subsequent breast carcinoma (see Table 23.1). However, these changes are also associated with a higher likelihood of finding “at-risk” lesions in the surrounding breast tissue (e.g., atypical hyperplasia) that may be the true drivers of increased risk.

Phyllodes Tumor

Phyllodes tumor, like fibroadenoma, arises from intralobular stroma but is much less common, accounting for approximately 2.5% of fibroepithelial lesions. Like fibroadenomas, the majority of phyllodes tumors have mutations in MED12 (part of the transcriptional regulator mediator complex) and RARA (retinoic acid receptor alpha, also involved in transcriptional regulation), suggesting a shared pathogenesis. However, phyllodes tumors are more likely to have mutations in additional genes such as TERT (the gene that encodes telomerase), TP53, and RB.

Most phyllodes tumors are detected as palpable masses, others by mammography. The tumors vary in size from a few centimeters to massive lesions involving the entire breast. The larger lesions often have bulbous protrusions (phyllodes is Greek for “leaf-like”) due to the presence of nodules of proliferating stroma covered by epithelium (Fig. 23.25A–B). In some tumors these protrusions extend into a cystic space. This growth pattern may also occasionally be seen in large fibroadenomas and is not an indication of malignancy. Phyllodes tumors are classified as benign, borderline, or malignant based on the histologic features of the stroma. Benign phyllodes tumors resemble fibroadenomas but their stroma is more cellular and mitotically active. Borderline phyllodes tumors demonstrate more prominent stromal atypia, cellularity, and mitotic activity compared to benign phyllodes tumors. Malignant phyllodes tumors are widely infiltrative and may be difficult to distinguish from sarcoma, particularly when there is marked stromal overgrowth with few residual epithelial elements.

Clinical Features

Most phyllodes tumors present in the sixth decade, 10 to 20 years later than the peak age for fibroadenoma. Up to 75% have benign cytologic features; these occasionally recur locally but do not metastasize. In contrast, borderline and malignant phyllodes tumors show an increased rate of recurrence, with margin status being the most important predictor of local recurrence following excision. Regardless of grade, lymphatic spread is rare, and axillary lymph node dissection is contraindicated.

The uncommon malignant lesions give rise to distant hematogenous metastases in about one-third of cases. Only the neoplastic stromal component is present in metastases.

Lesions of Interlobular Stroma

Tumors of the interlobular stroma of the breast are composed of stromal cells without an accompanying epithelial component. These include benign as well as malignant tumors, all uncommon and hence considered briefly.

Benign Lesions of Interlobular Stroma

Myofibroblastoma consists of myofibroblasts and is unusual in that it is the only breast tumor that is equally common in males. Lipomas are often palpable and can also be detected mammographically as fat-containing lesions. Fibromatosis is a clonal proliferation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts. It presents as an irregular, infiltrating mass that can involve muscle. Though locally aggressive, this lesion does not metastasize. Some cases are associated with prior trauma or surgery. Other cases occur as part of familial adenomatous polyposis, hereditary desmoid syndrome, and Gardner syndrome.

Malignant Lesions of Interlobular Stroma

Malignant stromal tumors of the breast are rare. The only sarcoma that occurs with any frequency in the breast is angiosarcoma; however, it accounts for less than 0.05% of breast malignancies. Angiosarcoma of the breast may be sporadic (primary) or arise as a complication of therapy (secondary). Most sporadic angiosarcomas occur in the breast parenchyma of young females (mean age 35 years) and have a poor prognosis. Tumors occurring after treatment for breast cancer typically arise in older females and are associated with radiation therapy or chronic lymphedema (Stewart-Treves syndrome). After radiation therapy, approximately 0.3% of females develop angiosarcomas in breast skin, with most cases being diagnosed 5 to 10 years after treatment. The molecular hallmark of secondary angiosarcoma is high-level MYC am- plification, which can be detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization and is present in more than 90% of cases.

Key concepts
  • Intralobular stroma is the origin of two biphasic tumors, fibroadenoma and phyllodes tumor, which are distinguished based on histological criteria such as stromal cellularity.
  • Fibroadenomas are the most common benign tumor of the breast.
  • Tumors of interlobular stroma consist only of stromal cells and include both benign and malignant lesions.
  • Angiosarcoma is the most common interlobular stromal malignancy and can either be sporadic or associated with radiation exposure or lymphedema.

07Other Malignant Tumors of the Breast

Hematolymphoid and cutaneous malignancies, together with breast metastases from other sites account for less than 5% of malignant breast tumors. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma may arise primarily in the breast, or the breasts may be secondarily involved by systemic disease.

Most primary breast lymphomas are of B-cell type, with the most common subtype being diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (Chapter 13).

While rare, T-cell lymphomas may arise in association with breast implants (breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma), possibly due to chronic inflammation, which is known to stimulate lymphoma development in other contexts. Young females with Burkitt lymphoma may present with massive bilateral breast involvement, often while pregnant or lactating. Malignant tumors may arise from the epidermis and dermis of breast skin; these tumors are identical to their counterparts found in skin elsewhere (see Chapter 25). Metastases to the breast are rare; the most common culprits are melanoma and ovarian carcinoma.
