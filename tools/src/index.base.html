    Define vasculitis. Types of vasculitis.

    Vasculitis: Inflammation of the blood vessel wall, with damage (often fibrinoid necrosis) that may lead to thrombosis, ischaemia or haemorrhage. It is mostly immune-mediated (immune complex deposition, ANCA, anti-endothelial cell antibodies, or T-cell reactions), or due to direct infection.

    Vessel sizeTypes
    Large vesselGiant cell (temporal) arteritis, Takayasu arteritis
    Medium vesselPolyarteritis nodosa, Kawasaki disease, Buerger disease (thromboangiitis obliterans)
    Small vessel: immune complexHenoch-Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis), cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis, hypersensitivity (leukocytoclastic) vasculitis
    Small vessel: ANCA-associatedGranulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener), microscopic polyangiitis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss)

    Define atherosclerosis. Tell me the pathogenesis of the development of an atherosclerotic plaque.

    Atherosclerosis: A pattern of vascular disease characterised by intimal lesions called atheromas, which, when advanced, protrude into vessel lumens, mechanically obstruct blood flow, and, after rupture, cause catastrophic formation of an obstructive thrombus. The name comes from the Greek for "gruel" and "hardening".

    Pathogenesis (sequence)
    1. Endothelial injury and dysfunction: increased vascular permeability, leukocyte adhesion and thrombosis.
    2. Lipoprotein accumulation: LDL and its oxidised forms accumulate in the vessel wall.
    3. Monocyte adhesion to the endothelium, migration into the intima, and transformation into macrophages and foam cells .
    4. Platelet adhesion.
    5. Factor release from activated platelets, macrophages and vascular wall cells, which recruits smooth muscle cells (SMCs) from the media or from circulating precursors.
    6. SMC proliferation , extracellular matrix (ECM) production and recruitment of T cells.
    7. Lipid accumulation both extracellularly and inside macrophages and SMCs.
    8. Calcification of ECM and necrotic debris, late in the process.
    B1

    Composition of an atheroma. Common sites of atheroma formation.

    Composition: Three components, arranged in a raised plaque.

    PartContents
    Fibrous capSMCs, macrophages, foam cells, lymphocytes, collagen, elastin and proteoglycans
    Necrotic (lipid) coreDead cells and debris, foam cells, extracellular lipid, cholesterol crystals, calcium
    Periphery (shoulder)Neovascularisation (small new vessels) and inflammatory cells

    The media beneath the plaque is thinned and atrophic.

    Common sites (in descending order)
    1. Lower abdominal aorta and iliac arteries
    2. Coronary arteries
    3. Popliteal arteries
    4. Internal carotid arteries
    5. Vessels of the circle of Willis

    Upper extremity vessels are usually spared. Mesenteric and renal arteries are usually spared, except at their ostia.

    FeatureArteriosclerosisAtherosclerosis
    MeaningGeneral term for "hardening of the arteries": thickening and loss of elasticity of the arterial wallA specific type of arteriosclerosis with intimal atheromas
    Vessels involvedArteries and arterioles of all sizesLarge and medium-sized elastic and muscular arteries
    LesionVaries with the typeAtheroma: fibrous cap over a lipid-rich necrotic core
    TypesAtherosclerosis, Mönckeberg medial calcific sclerosis, arteriolosclerosis (hyaline and hyperplastic)Single entity
    Lipid involvementNot necessarilyCentral feature (LDL, foam cells)
    AssociationsHypertension, diabetes, ageingHyperlipidaemia, smoking, hypertension, diabetes
    Complications of atherosclerosis
    • Mechanical obstruction of blood flow (chronic ischaemia)

    • Increased diffusion distance from the lumen to the media, causing smooth muscle cell ischaemia, wall weakening and aneurysm formation

    Clinical consequences: Myocardial infarction, stroke, abdominal aortic aneurysm, peripheral arterial disease with gangrene, mesenteric ischaemia, and atheroembolism.

    CategoryConditions

    What is the etiopathogenesis of rheumatic fever? Diagnostic criteria of rheumatic fever.

    Aetiology: Group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus ( S. pyogenes ) pharyngitis, occurring a few weeks earlier.

    Pathogenesis
    1. Group A streptococcal pharyngitis.
    2. Immune response develops against streptococcal antigens, especially M protein, with antibodies and CD4+ T cells. The 2-3 week latent period is the time needed for this adaptive response.
    3. Molecular mimicry: M protein shares antigens with cardiac myosin, cardiac and valvular glycoproteins, myocardial sarcolemma, and joint and connective tissue antigens, so the antibodies and T cells cross-react with host tissue.
    4. Antibody-mediated injury: complement activation and recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages cause inflammation.
    5. T-cell-mediated injury: cytokine release activates macrophages and forms Aschoff bodies in the myocardium.
    6. Damage to myocardium, endocardium and valves, and to joints and connective tissue.
    7. Healing and scarring lead to chronic fibrotic valve lesions, called rheumatic heart disease.

    Diagnostic criteria (revised Jones): Evidence of preceding group A streptococcal infection (raised ASO titre, positive throat culture or rapid antigen test), plus 2 major, or 1 major and 2 minor manifestations.

    Major criteriaMinor criteria
    Migratory polyarthritis of large jointsFever
    Pancarditis (myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis)Arthralgia
    Subcutaneous nodulesRaised ESR or CRP
    Erythema marginatumPrevious rheumatic fever
    Sydenham choreaLeukocytosis
    First-degree AV block
    B1

    Which valve is commonly affected by rheumatic fever? What is an Aschoff body?

    Valves: Mitral valve (60-70%), aortic valve (about 25%).

    Aschoff body: The characteristic (pathognomonic) lesion of rheumatic carditis. It is a focal inflammatory lesion in the interstitial connective tissue of the myocardium, often around blood vessels, and is found in all three layers of the heart.

    • Central fibrinoid necrosis .
    • Surrounded by T lymphocytes, occasional plasma cells, and activated macrophages.
    • Macrophages include Anitschkow cells (abundant cytoplasm, central nucleus with slender wavy chromatin ribbon, called "caterpillar cells") and multinucleated Aschoff giant cells .
    • Evolves from an early exudative stage to a proliferative (granulomatous) stage, and heals by fibrosis.

    What is an R-S cell? Variants of the R-S cell. Morphology of a classical R-S cell.

    RS cell: The neoplastic cell of Hodgkin lymphoma, derived from germinal centre B cells. It is large, and is surrounded by reactive lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils and histiocytes.

    Morphology of classical RS cell
    • Large cell (15 to 45 µm) with abundant, slightly eosinophilic or amphophilic cytoplasm.
    • Binucleate or bilobed nucleus with a "mirror image" arrangement, giving an "owl-eye" appearance.
    • Thin nuclear membrane with vesicular (open) chromatin.
    • Each nucleus or lobe has a large, prominent, eosinophilic inclusion-like nucleolus surrounded by a clear halo.
    • Immunophenotype: CD15+, CD30+ , PAX5 weak+, CD45 negative.

    What are esophageal varices? Mention their mechanism.

    Dilated, tortuous submucosal veins in the lower esophagus, due to portal hypertension.

    Mechanism

    • Portal hypertension (commonest cause is cirrhosis) raises the pressure in the portal system.
    • Blood is diverted through the left gastric (coronary) vein into the esophageal submucosal veins, which drain to the azygos system (portosystemic anastomosis).

    • Rupture, causing massive haematemesis and melaena
    • Hypovolaemic shock and death
    • Recurrent bleeding
    • Precipitation of hepatic encephalopathy
    • Aspiration pneumonia

    B4

    Tell the pathogenesis of acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. What is AGN? Mention the role of streptococcal infection in the pathogenesis of AGN.

    1. Infection with a nephritogenic strain of group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus (throat M types 1, 4, 12; skin M types 47, 49, 55, 57, 60).
    2. Streptococcal antigens (e.g. NAPlr, SpeB) are released.
    3. Antibodies form against them 1 to 4 weeks later (type III hypersensitivity).
    4. Immune complexes form in the circulation or in situ (planted antigen) and deposit in the subepithelial space of the glomerular basement membrane.
    5. Complement is activated (alternative pathway), so C3 falls.
    6. Neutrophils and monocytes infiltrate and release mediators, causing endothelial and mesangial proliferation.
    7. The result is a diffusely hypercellular glomerulus, with granular IgG and C3 on immunofluorescence and subepithelial humps on electron microscopy.
    8. Reduced GFR causes oliguria, salt and water retention, oedema and hypertension. Basement membrane damage causes haematuria and proteinuria.
    AGN and the role of streptococcal infection

    AGN (acute glomerulonephritis): Sudden onset of nephritic syndrome, most often post-streptococcal in children.

    Role of streptococcal infection: It is the trigger. Group A beta-haemolytic streptococci from a sore throat or skin infection (pyoderma) produce antigens that provoke antibody formation. The resulting immune complexes deposit in the glomeruli, activate complement and cause inflammation. Symptoms begin about 1 to 3 weeks after the infection, and the disease is not caused by direct bacterial invasion of the kidney.

    What is endometriosis and adenomyosis? Describe the pathogenesis of endometriosis.

    Pancreas / Diabetes Mellitus

    B8

    What is hypoglycemia? Which one is more dangerous: hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia? Explain.

    Hypoglycaemia: A low blood glucose level, generally below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL), with clinically significant hypoglycaemia below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL). Diagnosis is confirmed by Whipple's triad : symptoms of hypoglycaemia, a low plasma glucose at the time, and relief of symptoms when glucose is raised.

    Which is more dangerous?

    Acute hypoglycaemia is more dangerous in the short term. Chronic hyperglycaemia is more dangerous in the long term. Hypoglycaemia is an emergency because it starves the brain within minutes and can kill or disable quickly, and this is the main limiting factor in tight diabetes control. Hyperglycaemia is usually less immediately lethal, but sustained high glucose slowly damages blood vessels and nerves and causes most of diabetes-related morbidity and mortality.

    B8

    What is glycosylated hemoglobin? Tell its importance.

    Glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c): Haemoglobin that has glucose irreversibly attached to it (non-enzymatic glycation of the beta chain of HbA). The amount formed depends on the average blood glucose level over the lifespan of the red cell (about 120 days), so it reflects the average glucose over the previous 2 to 3 months.

    Importance
    • Monitoring glycaemic control: Shows long-term control in diabetes and is not affected by day-to-day fluctuations or recent meals. It is measured every 3 months.
    • Diagnosis of diabetes: HbA1c of 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or above. Prediabetes is 5.7 to 6.4% (39 to 47 mmol/mol).
    • Treatment target: Usually below 7% (53 mmol/mol) for most patients, to reduce microvascular and macrovascular complications.
    • Assessing risk: Higher HbA1c predicts a greater risk of retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy and cardiovascular disease.
    • Evaluating treatment response: Helps decide whether therapy needs to be adjusted.
    • Distinguishing chronic from stress hyperglycaemia: A normal HbA1c with high glucose in an acutely ill patient suggests stress hyperglycaemia.

    Limitation: Unreliable in conditions altering red cell lifespan, such as haemolytic anaemia, recent blood loss or transfusion (falsely low), iron deficiency anaemia (falsely high), and haemoglobinopathies.

    What is OGTT? Discuss its procedure. Name the indications for OGTT. What are renal and alimentary glycosuria?

    OGTT (Oral Glucose Tolerance Test): A test that measures the body's ability to handle a standard oral glucose load, by measuring plasma glucose before and after the load.

    Procedure
    1. Preparation: The patient eats an unrestricted, carbohydrate-rich diet (at least 150 to 200 g per day) for 3 days before the test, and continues normal physical activity.
    2. Fasting: Overnight fast of 8 to 14 hours (water allowed). No smoking, and stop interfering drugs if possible (steroids, thiazides, etc.).
    3. Fasting sample: A venous blood sample is taken for fasting plasma glucose.
    4. Glucose load: 75 g of anhydrous glucose dissolved in 250 to 300 mL of water, drunk within 5 minutes. Dose in children is 1.75 g/kg body weight (maximum 75 g).
    5. Rest: The patient sits quietly and does not eat, drink or smoke during the test.
    6. Post-load sample: Blood is taken again at 2 hours (in some protocols also at 30, 60 and 90 minutes).
    7. Urine samples may also be tested for glucose.
    Interpretation (WHO)

    Category Fasting plasma glucose 2-hour plasma glucose

    NormalBelow 6.1 mmol/LBelow 7.8 mmol/L
    Impaired fasting glucose6.1 to 6.9 mmol/LBelow 7.8 mmol/L
    Impaired glucose toleranceBelow 7.0 mmol/L7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L

    Indications for OGTT
    • Borderline or equivocal fasting or random glucose values
    • Suspected diabetes with normal fasting glucose
    • Screening for gestational diabetes (24 to 28 weeks)
    • Diagnosis of impaired glucose tolerance and impaired fasting glucose
    • Glycosuria with normal blood glucose (to distinguish renal glycosuria)
    • Family history of diabetes, or unexplained neuropathy, retinopathy or nephropathy
    • Assessment in acromegaly (GH suppression test)
    • Investigation of suspected reactive hypoglycaemia or malabsorption (flat curve)
    Renal and alimentary glycosuria

    Renal glycosuria: Glucose appears in urine despite a normal blood glucose level , because of a low renal threshold for glucose (a defect in tubular reabsorption, often due to SGLT2 mutation). It is benign and familial, and the OGTT curve is normal. It is also seen in pregnancy.

    Alimentary glycosuria: Transient glycosuria after a meal or glucose load, because of rapid absorption of glucose from the gut. The blood glucose rises briefly above the renal threshold (about 10 mmol/L), then falls to normal by 2 hours. It is seen after gastrectomy, gastrojejunostomy and in thyrotoxicosis. The OGTT shows an early sharp peak (lag storage curve) with a normal 2-hour value.

    What is diabetic ketoacidosis? How does diabetic ketoacidosis develop in a case of uncontrolled diabetes?

    Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): An acute, life-threatening metabolic emergency, mainly in type 1 diabetes, characterised by the triad of hyperglycaemia (glucose above 11 mmol/L), ketonaemia/ketonuria , and metabolic acidosis (pH below 7.3, bicarbonate below 15 mmol/L).

    Development of DKA in uncontrolled diabetes
    1. Absolute insulin deficiency (missed insulin, infection, or other stress) together with a rise in counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, cortisol, adrenaline, growth hormone).
    2. Hyperglycaemia:
      • Glucose uptake by muscle and fat falls.
      • The liver increases glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
      • The blood glucose rises sharply.
    3. Osmotic diuresis:
      • Glucose exceeds the renal threshold, causing glycosuria.
      • Loss of water and electrolytes (Na ⁺ , K ⁺ ) leads to dehydration, hypovolaemia and hyperosmolality.
    4. Lipolysis: Lack of insulin removes the inhibition of hormone-sensitive lipase, so fat breakdown increases and large amounts of free fatty acids are released.
    5. Ketogenesis:
      • In the liver, free fatty acids undergo beta-oxidation to produce acetyl-CoA.
      • Glucagon promotes entry of fatty acids into mitochondria.
      • Because oxaloacetate is diverted to gluconeogenesis, the acetyl-CoA cannot enter the Krebs cycle and is converted to ketone bodies (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetone).
    6. Metabolic acidosis: Acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate are strong acids. They overwhelm the buffering capacity, causing a high anion gap metabolic acidosis.
    7. Consequences:
      • Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid respiration to blow off CO ₂ )
      • Acetone smell on the breath
      • Nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain
      • Dehydration, hypotension and tachycardia
      • Drowsiness progressing to coma
      • Total body potassium depletion (serum K ⁺ may be normal or high initially due to acidosis, and falls with insulin treatment)

    Tell the pathogenesis of type-2 diabetes mellitus.

    Pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes mellitus
    Step 1: Predisposing factors
    • Genetic susceptibility (strong family history, polygenic).
    • Environmental factors: obesity (especially central or visceral), sedentary lifestyle, high-calorie diet, ageing.
    Step 2: Insulin resistance
    • Target tissues (skeletal muscle, liver, adipose tissue) respond poorly to insulin.
    • Causes include excess free fatty acids and adipokines from visceral fat (raised TNF-α, IL-6, resistin; reduced adiponectin), and defects in insulin receptor signalling (post-receptor defects).
    • Result: reduced glucose uptake in muscle and fat, and failure of insulin to suppress hepatic glucose output.
    Step 3: Compensatory hyperinsulinaemia
    • Beta cells secrete more insulin to overcome resistance.
    • Blood glucose stays normal or near normal at this stage (prediabetes or impaired glucose tolerance).
    Step 4: Beta cell dysfunction and failure
    • Over time the beta cells cannot sustain the increased demand.
    • Contributing mechanisms: glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, amyloid (islet amyloid polypeptide) deposition, oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress, and reduced beta cell mass.
    • Insulin secretion becomes inadequate (relative insulin deficiency), and the first-phase insulin response is lost.
    Step 5: Increased hepatic glucose production
    • Persistent insulin resistance in the liver and raised glucagon lead to increased gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, causing fasting hyperglycaemia.
    Step 6: Established hyperglycaemia
    • Fasting and post-prandial glucose rise, leading to clinical diabetes.
    • Ketosis is uncommon because a small amount of insulin remains and is enough to suppress lipolysis and ketogenesis.
    Step 7: Contribution of the gut hormones
    • Reduced incretin effect (GLP-1 deficiency or resistance), further impairing insulin secretion.
    Step 8: Chronic complications
    • Sustained hyperglycaemia causes non-enzymatic glycation, polyol pathway activation and oxidative stress, damaging small and large vessels (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, and macrovascular disease).

    Structure of the skin:

    Three layers: epidermis, dermis and hypodermis.

    1. Epidermis:

    Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, no blood vessels. Cells: keratinocytes (about 90%), melanocytes, Langerhans cells, Merkel cells.

    • Reticular dermis: dense irregular connective tissue with collagen and elastic fibres; hair follicles, sweat and sebaceous glands, nerves, vessels

    3. Hypodermis (subcutis):

    Loose connective tissue and fat; insulation, energy storage, cushioning, anchors skin to deeper structures.

    Functions: protection (barrier), thermoregulation, sensation, vitamin D synthesis, excretion, immunity, prevention of water loss.

    Histopathological terms:

    • Parakeratosis: retention of nuclei in the stratum corneum (incomplete maturation), usually with a reduced or absent granular layer. Seen in psoriasis, chronic eczema, actinic keratosis, seborrhoeic dermatitis

    • Acanthosis: thickened stratum spinosum, making the epidermis thicker; regular (psoriasiform) or irregular. Seen in psoriasis, chronic eczema, lichen simplex chronicus, acanthosis nigricans
    • Spongiosis: intercellular oedema between keratinocytes (sponge-like); can progress to intraepidermal vesicles. Hallmark of eczema and allergic contact dermatitis

    Osteomyelitis
    Definition

    Osteomyelitis is inflammation of bone and bone marrow, usually caused by infection (most often bacterial). It may involve the medullary cavity, cortex and periosteum.

    Classification
    By duration/course
    • Acute (pyogenic): neutrophilic infiltrate, usually within the first few weeks
    • Subacute
    • Chronic : lymphocytes and plasma cells, with bone destruction and repair going on together
    By causative organism / type
    • Pyogenic (bacterial)
    • Non-pyogenic : tuberculous, fungal, syphilitic, viral
    Causes
    • Staphylococcus aureus is the commonest cause (about 80-90% of pyogenic cases).
    • Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella : seen in IV drug users, urinary tract infection and the elderly.
    • Salmonella : classically in sickle cell disease.
    • Haemophilus influenzae and group B streptococci : neonates.
    • Mixed organisms, including anaerobes : after open fractures or with diabetic foot.
    • Mycobacterium tuberculosis : tuberculous osteomyelitis, e.g. Pott's disease of the spine.
    Routes of Entry of Bacteria
    1. Haematogenous spread (commonest, especially in children). It arises from a distant focus such as skin boils or the respiratory or urinary tract. Bacteria settle in the metaphysis, where blood flow is slow and sluggish.
    2. Direct implantation : open (compound) fractures, penetrating wounds, surgery or prosthetic implants.
    3. Contiguous spread from adjacent infected tissue, such as cellulitis, diabetic foot ulcer, or sinusitis and dental infection.
    Morphology of Chronic Osteomyelitis

    It develops when acute infection is untreated or inadequately treated.

    • Bone: dead bone ( sequestrum ) surrounded by a shell of new reactive bone ( involucrum ). The bone shows a mixture of destruction and new bone formation.
    • Cavity and sinuses: pus collects and drains through sinus tracts (draining sinuses) to the skin surface.
    • Microscopy: chronic inflammatory cells (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages), fibrosis of marrow spaces, granulation tissue and reactive new bone formation.
    • Periosteum: thickened, with subperiosteal new bone.
    • Complications include a persistent discharging sinus, pathological fracture, secondary amyloidosis, septic arthritis, and rarely squamous cell carcinoma developing in the sinus tract.
    Definitions
    • Sequestrum: a fragment of dead (necrotic) bone lying within pus or granulation tissue, separated from living bone. It acts as a source of persistent infection because antibiotics cannot reach it, and it often needs surgical removal.
    • Involucrum: a sheath of new living bone formed by the periosteum around the sequestrum. It may have openings called cloacae through which pus drains.
    • Brodie's abscess: a small, localized subacute intraosseous abscess, usually in the metaphysis of a long bone (classically the tibia) in children and young adults. It is surrounded by dense sclerotic bone, often has little systemic upset, and may present with pain, especially at night. Radiologically it appears as a radiolucent lesion with a sclerotic rim .

    What is Pott's disease?

    Pott's disease is tuberculosis of the spine (tuberculous spondylitis). It is not a tumor, though it can look like one on imaging, so it is often part of the differential diagnosis.

    • Cause: Mycobacterium tuberculosis reaches the vertebrae, usually by spreading through the blood from a focus elsewhere, often the lungs.
    • Site: Most often the lower thoracic and upper lumbar spine.
    • Pathology: The infection typically starts in the vertebral body next to the disc, then destroys bone and the intervertebral disc. The vertebrae collapse and produce an angular curve called a gibbus deformity (kyphosis). A cold abscess (a "cold" paraspinal or psoas abscess) can form without the usual signs of acute inflammation.
    • Symptoms: Chronic back pain, stiffness, low-grade fever, night sweats, weight loss and, in advanced disease, neurological deficits such as leg weakness or paraplegia ( Pott's paraplegia ) from cord compression.
    • Diagnosis: MRI is the most sensitive imaging test. X-ray shows disc space narrowing and vertebral destruction. Confirmation comes from CT-guided biopsy, culture or PCR (for example GeneXpert).
    • Treatment: Prolonged anti-tubercular therapy (usually 9 to 12 months, sometimes longer), with surgery for spinal instability, severe deformity, abscess drainage or progressive neurological deficit.

    Name the bone-forming tumors. Describe the morphology of osteosarcoma. What is the common site of origin of osteosarcoma? Describe the morphology of osteosarcoma. What are the radiological findings?

    Bone-forming (osteogenic) tumors
    • Benign: osteoma, osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma
    • Malignant: osteosarcoma (osteogenic sarcoma)
    Common site of origin of osteosarcoma
    • Most often arises in the metaphysis of long bones , especially around the knee : the distal femur (most common), then the proximal tibia, followed by the proximal humerus.
    • Less often in the pelvis, jaw or spine (more typical of older patients).
    • Peak age is 10 to 20 years (adolescent growth spurt), with a second smaller peak in older adults, usually secondary to Paget's disease, radiation or bone infarcts.
    Morphology
    Gross
    • A large, bulky, gritty, tan-white tumor, often with hemorrhage and cystic degeneration.
    • It usually begins in the metaphysis, destroys the cortex and extends into the medullary cavity.
    • It lifts the periosteum and spreads into the surrounding soft tissue.
    • It commonly spares the epiphyseal cartilage plate, though it may occasionally cross it.
    • It can spread along the marrow cavity and may produce "skip lesions."
    Microscopic
    • Malignant, pleomorphic, spindle-shaped or polygonal cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, atypical mitoses and bizarre giant cells.
    • The diagnostic feature is direct production of neoplastic osteoid or bone by the tumor cells (lace-like, irregular osteoid).
    • Variable areas of malignant cartilage or fibrous tissue may be present.
    • Subtypes include osteoblastic, chondroblastic, fibroblastic, telangiectatic and small cell types.
    Radiological findings
    • Mixed lytic and sclerotic lesion in the metaphysis, with poorly defined margins and a "moth-eaten" or permeative pattern.
    • Codman's triangle: a triangular elevation of periosteum at the edge of the tumor where new bone forms.
    • "Sunburst" (sunray) appearance: radiating spicules of new bone perpendicular to the cortex.
    • Cortical destruction with a soft-tissue mass, often containing cloud-like tumor bone.
    • MRI is best for defining intramedullary and soft-tissue extent and skip lesions.
    • CT chest and bone scan are used to look for metastases, most often to the lungs.

    Note: Codman's triangle and the sunburst pattern are characteristic but not specific, as they can also be seen in Ewing sarcoma and some other conditions.

    Classify bone tumors. Describe the morphology of giant cell tumor of bone.

    Morphology of giant cell tumor of bone
    Gross
    • A large, soft, red-brown (chocolate-colored) tumor, friable and often hemorrhagic.
    • It typically arises in the epiphysis of long bones (distal femur, proximal tibia, distal radius), extending toward the metaphysis after the growth plate closes.
    • It expands and erodes the bone, thinning the cortex, and may break through into the soft tissue, often held in by a thin shell of reactive bone.
    • Areas of hemorrhage, necrosis and cystic degeneration are common. Secondary aneurysmal bone cyst change may be present.
    Microscopic
    • Two cell populations:
      • Mononuclear stromal cells (round to spindle-shaped), the true neoplastic component, with uniform oval nuclei and occasional mitoses.
      • Multinucleated osteoclast-type giant cells , numerous and evenly scattered, with many nuclei (often 20 or more) that resemble the nuclei of the stromal cells. These are reactive cells recruited by RANKL expressed by the stromal cells.
    • The nuclei of the stromal cells are similar to those of the giant cells, and atypia is minimal.
    • Foci of necrosis, hemorrhage, hemosiderin and foamy macrophages may be seen. Reactive woven bone may form at the periphery.
    • Malignant transformation is uncommon (under 2%), but the tumor can recur locally and rarely metastasizes to the lungs.

    Molecular note: Most cases carry a mutation in the H3F3A gene (G34W), and the tumor is treated with curettage, or with denosumab (a RANKL inhibitor) when surgery is difficult.

    Classify bone tumors. What is Ewing sarcoma?

    Ewing sarcoma

    A highly malignant primary bone tumor of small round blue cells , belonging to the Ewing sarcoma family of tumors together with primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET). It is the second most common bone sarcoma in children and adolescents after osteosarcoma.

    Epidemiology
    • Peak age is 10-15 years, and it is uncommon after 30.
    • More frequent in males.
    • Rare in people of African descent.
    Genetics
    • Characteristic translocation t(11;22)(q24;q12) , producing the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion gene (seen in about 85% of cases). Less often t(21;22), giving EWSR1-ERG.
    • Tumor cells express CD99 (MIC2) , which is a useful immunohistochemical marker.
    Site
    • Arises in the diaphysis (shaft) or metadiaphysis of long bones, especially the femur, tibia and humerus.
    • Also flat bones such as the pelvis, ribs and scapula.
    • Can occur in soft tissue (extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma).
    Morphology
    • Gross: Soft, tan-white, often hemorrhagic and necrotic tumor arising in the medullary cavity, spreading through the cortex to form a soft-tissue mass and lifting the periosteum.
    • Microscopic: Sheets of uniform small, round, blue cells with scant clear or pale cytoplasm (rich in glycogen , PAS positive) and round nuclei with fine chromatin. There is no osteoid production. Homer Wright rosettes (tumor cells around a central fibrillary space) may be seen.
    Clinical features
    • Pain, swelling and a palpable mass, often with fever, weight loss, anemia, raised ESR and leukocytosis , which can mimic osteomyelitis.
    • Often metastatic at diagnosis (lungs, bone, bone marrow).
    Radiology
    • Destructive, permeative (moth-eaten) lytic lesion in the diaphysis.
    • "Onion-skin" periosteal reaction (lamellated new bone), and sometimes Codman's triangle.
    • A large soft-tissue mass; MRI shows the full extent.
    Treatment and prognosis
    • Multi-agent chemotherapy, followed by surgery and/or radiotherapy for local control (the tumor is radiosensitive).
    • The 5-year survival is about 70% for localized disease, but much lower with metastases.

    Soft tissue

    Soft tissue refers to the non-epithelial, extraskeletal tissues of the body that connect, support or surround other structures and organs. It includes:

    • Fibrous tissue (tendons, ligaments, fascia)
    • Fat (adipose tissue)

    What are the special characteristics of CNS tumors?
    • Clinical course depends more on growth pattern and location than on histologic classification as benign or malignant.
    • Diffuse gliomas widely infiltrate brain parenchyma, making them amenable to complete surgical resection without compromising neurologic function.
    • Even benign tumors and low-grade malignancies can cause serious clinical deficits and may prove fatal. Any CNS neoplasm, regardless of histologic grade or classification, may be lethal if situated in a critical brain region (e.g., a benign posterior fossa meningioma may cause cardiorespiratory arrest by compressing vital centers in the medulla).
    • Even the most highly malignant gliomas rarely metastasize outside the CNS.
    • Some malignant pediatric tumors spread through the CSF when they encroach on the subarachnoid space, resulting in cerebrospinal dissemination far from the original tumor site.
    Meningioma

    A tumor of the meninges , arising from the arachnoid cap cells (arachnoid villi) that cover the brain and spinal cord. It is the most common primary intracranial tumor in adults after metastases, and is usually benign (WHO grade 1).

    Epidemiology and risk factors
    • Peak age is 50 to 60 years, and it is more common in women (about 2:1). Tumor cells often express progesterone receptors , and growth may accelerate in pregnancy.
    • Ionizing radiation to the head is a risk factor.
    • Loss of chromosome 22 ( NF2 gene) is the commonest genetic change. Meningiomas are frequent in neurofibromatosis type 2 (often multiple).
    Sites
    • Parasagittal region, falx cerebri, cerebral convexities, sphenoid ridge, olfactory groove, and spinal canal (thoracic region, especially in women).
    Gross
    • Round or lobulated, firm, well-circumscribed, dural-based mass that compresses the brain rather than invading it.
    • Sometimes a flat, plaque-like growth ( en plaque ), especially along the sphenoid ridge. May invade the overlying skull, causing hyperostosis .
    Microscopic
    • Meningothelial (syncytial) type: whorls of cells with indistinct borders.
    • Fibroblastic , transitional and psammomatous types. Concentric whorls and psammoma bodies (laminated calcified concentric structures) are characteristic.
    • Cells are EMA and vimentin positive, and many are progesterone-receptor positive.
    • Atypical (grade 2) and anaplastic or malignant (grade 3) forms show increased mitoses, necrosis and brain invasion, with a higher recurrence rate.
    Clinical features
    • Slowly growing, so it often presents late with headache, seizures, focal neurological deficits or visual problems depending on the site. Many are found incidentally.
    Radiology
    • CT: well-defined, hyperdense extra-axial mass, often calcified, with bone changes.
    • MRI: extra-axial mass with strong homogeneous contrast enhancement and a "dural tail" .
    Treatment
    • Surgical resection (curative if complete). Radiotherapy or radiosurgery for residual, recurrent or higher-grade tumors, or when surgery is not possible.

    What is retinoblastoma? Morphology of retinoblastoma.

    Retinoblastoma

    The most common primary intraocular malignancy of childhood , arising from primitive retinal neuroblasts (photoreceptor precursor cells) .

    Morphology
    Gross
    • Creamy white to pink, friable tumor arising in the retina.
    • Endophytic growth: projects into the vitreous, with vitreous seeding.
    • Exophytic growth: grows beneath the retina, causing retinal detachment.
    • Diffuse infiltrating type: spreads along the retina without a mass.
    • Areas of necrosis and calcification are common. Multifocal in hereditary cases.
    • Spread: along the optic nerve into the brain, through the sclera into the orbit, and by blood to bone marrow and bone.
    Microscopic
    • Small round blue cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, scanty cytoplasm and a high N:C ratio, in sheets.
    • Extensive necrosis with basophilic calcification (DNA from necrotic cells deposited around blood vessels).
    • Pseudorosettes: viable cells cuffing blood vessels, surrounded by necrotic tumor.
    • Rosettes:
      • Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes (highly specific): cells arranged around a central lumen, showing early photoreceptor differentiation.
      • Homer Wright rosettes: cells around a central tangle of fibrils (also seen in neuroblastoma, Ewing sarcoma).
      • Fleurettes: flower-shaped clusters showing photoreceptor differentiation (benign-appearing).

    • Endometrioid adenocarcinoma of the endometrium
    • Endometrial polyp

    • Chronic endometritis (including tuberculous)

    A large testicular mass is received after radical orchiectomy, with clinical information of a feeling of heaviness and gradual enlargement of the painless mass. Which germ cell tumor commonly presents with such a clinical presentation? Describe the histomorphology of this tumor.

    Tumour: Seminoma (commonest testicular germ cell tumour, peak age 30 to 40).

    Histomorphology
    • Gross: Large, well-circumscribed, homogeneous, cream-white, lobulated, fleshy mass with a bulging cut surface. Necrosis and haemorrhage are usually absent, and the tunica albuginea is intact.
    • Microscopy:
      • Sheets and lobules of uniform large polygonal cells with distinct cell borders
      • Clear, glycogen-rich cytoplasm
      • Central round nuclei with prominent nucleoli
      • Fibrous septa infiltrated by lymphocytes (sometimes with granulomas)
    • Immunohistochemistry: OCT3/4, PLAP and CD117 positive.
    • It spreads through lymphatics to para-aortic nodes, and is highly radiosensitive.

    A 15-year-old female has complaints of lower abdominal pain. She underwent USG, and an ovarian mass was found. What is the most common ovarian tumor found in this age group? Describe the histomorphology of this tumor.

    Most common tumour at this age: Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst) , a germ cell tumour.

    Histomorphology
    • Gross: Unilocular cyst filled with sebaceous material and hair, with a solid protuberance (Rokitansky nodule) that may contain teeth or bone.
    • Microscopy: Mature tissues derived from all three germ layers:
      • Ectoderm: keratinising squamous epithelium, sebaceous glands, hair follicles, neural tissue
      • Mesoderm: fat, smooth muscle, bone, cartilage
      • Endoderm: respiratory and gastrointestinal epithelium, thyroid tissue
    • Complications: torsion, rupture, and rare malignant change (squamous cell carcinoma).

    A well-defined nodular mass is received following lumpectomy of a young female patient.

    Probable diagnosis: Fibroadenoma (differentials: phyllodes tumour, fibrocystic change, cyst).

    Histopathology
    • Gross: Well-circumscribed, encapsulated, firm, rubbery, grey-white, bulging mass with a whorled cut surface.
    • Microscopy: Proliferation of both glandular (epithelial) and stromal components:
      • Pericanalicular pattern: stroma surrounding open, round glands
      • Intracanalicular pattern: stroma compressing glands into slit-like spaces
      • Glands are lined by two layers (luminal epithelial cells and myoepithelial cells)
      • Loose, myxoid stroma with no atypia and rare mitoses

    A breast specimen is received after radical mastectomy of the right breast, showing an ulcerated growth on the skin, and the cut section shows a whitish mass lesion. What is the most probable diagnosis? Describe the histopathological features of this lesion.

    Most probable diagnosis: Invasive ductal carcinoma (no special type) of the breast.

    Histopathology
    • Gross: Hard, irregular, stellate mass with chalky-white streaks, infiltrating margins, skin ulceration, and possible nipple retraction.
    • Microscopy:
      • Malignant epithelial cells arranged in cords, nests, glands and sheets, infiltrating a desmoplastic stroma
      • Pleomorphic hyperchromatic nuclei, mitoses, and sometimes necrosis
      • An associated ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) component may be present
      • Lymphovascular and perineural invasion may be seen
    • Grading: Nottingham system (tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, mitotic count).
    • Immunohistochemistry: ER, PR, HER2 and Ki-67.

    An ulcerated lesion over the left lower leg of an elderly patient with foul-smelling discharge is found during clinical examination. What type of malignancy would you suspect here, and where will it metastasize?

    Suspected malignancy: Squamous cell carcinoma (as a Marjolin ulcer if it arose in a chronic ulcer, scar or burn).

    Metastasis: Mainly through lymphatics to the regional lymph nodes (popliteal and inguinal nodes). Distant haematogenous spread (lung, bone) is late and uncommon.

    A specimen following curettage biopsy of the shin of the left tibia is sent to our histopathology laboratory, with clinical information of a discharging sinus for the last 4 months. What is the most probable diagnosis? Mention the points in favor of your diagnosis.

    Most probable diagnosis: Chronic osteomyelitis (pyogenic, usually Staphylococcus aureus ).

    Points in favour
    • Long-standing discharging sinus with intermittent pain, swelling and fever
    • Site in a long bone (tibia)
    • X-ray: lytic areas with sclerosis, periosteal reaction, sequestrum (dead bone) and involucrum (new bone)
    • Raised ESR and CRP
    • Histology: necrotic bone fragments (sequestrum), granulation tissue, lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages, fibrosis and reactive new bone
    • Pus culture positive
    • AFB stain is done to exclude tuberculous osteomyelitis

    A 12-year-old boy presents with a mass over the upper part of the right tibia. X-ray shows a bone-forming mass with a sun-ray appearance. What is the most probable diagnosis? Describe the histopathological features of this tumor.

    Most probable diagnosis: Osteosarcoma.

    Histopathology
    • Malignant, pleomorphic spindle to polygonal cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, atypical mitoses and bizarre giant cells
    • Diagnostic feature: direct production of neoplastic osteoid or bone (lace-like) by tumour cells

    A 10-year-old boy presents with headache and vomiting for the last 3 days. CT scan of the brain shows a space-occupying lesion. What could be the most probable cause of this lesion? Mention the fate/prognosis of this case.

    Most probable cause: Medulloblastoma (posterior fossa tumour of the cerebellum). It obstructs the fourth ventricle, causing hydrocephalus and raised intracranial pressure. Pilocytic astrocytoma is the main alternative.

    Fate and prognosis
    • Highly malignant (WHO grade 4) small round blue cell tumour that spreads through the CSF ("drop metastases").
    • It is radiosensitive and chemosensitive. With surgery, craniospinal radiotherapy and chemotherapy, the 5-year survival is about 70 to 80% in average-risk patients.
    • Prognosis is worse with metastasis at diagnosis, very young age, large cell or anaplastic histology, or MYC amplification.
    • For pilocytic astrocytoma, prognosis is excellent (5-year survival over 90%).

    A specimen is received after right hemicolectomy, showing an ulcerated lesion encircling the lumen. On cut section, it shows a variegated appearance. What is the most likely diagnosis? Describe the histopathological features of this lesion.

    Most likely diagnosis: Adenocarcinoma of the colon.

    Histopathology
    • Malignant glands lined by tall columnar cells with hyperchromatic, pleomorphic, stratified nuclei, loss of polarity and mitoses
    • Back-to-back and cribriform glands, with necrotic debris in the lumen ("dirty necrosis")
    • Infiltration into the muscularis propria and pericolic fat, with a desmoplastic stroma
    • Grade: well (over 95% gland formation), moderate (50 to 95%), poor (below 50%)
    • Variants: mucinous (over 50% extracellular mucin) and signet ring cell types
    • Look for lymphovascular and perineural invasion, margin status and lymph node metastasis
    • Immunohistochemistry: CK20+, CDX2+, CK7-

    An obese 55-year-old male has complaints of recurrent, transient substernal chest pain during exercise, which is relieved by rest. What are the probable causes, and what investigations would you suggest to confirm the diagnosis?

    Probable cause: Stable angina pectoris due to fixed atherosclerotic stenosis of the coronary arteries (ischaemic heart disease). Other causes to exclude are aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and severe anaemia.

    Investigations
    • Resting ECG: may be normal, or show ST depression or T wave inversion
    • Exercise ECG (treadmill test): ST depression of 1 mm or more
    • Stress echocardiography or myocardial perfusion scan
    • CT coronary angiography, and coronary angiography (gold standard)
    • Blood tests: lipid profile, fasting glucose or HbA1c, CBC (anaemia), thyroid function, renal function
    • Serial troponin, to exclude acute coronary syndrome (normal in stable angina)
    • Chest X-ray
